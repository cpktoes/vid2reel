How to read this

Linear, start to finish. Read Part I, then do Parts II to V in order.

This guide is in the order you will actually work. Part I is reading only: what the tool is, how the build works, the parts it is made of, the road ahead, and what it costs. Nothing to do on the computer. Part II is day one: set up the Mac, create the project, join two clips with your own code, put it on GitHub, make a reel from a phone, then start GSD and learn the rituals every later stage follows. Part III builds the first complete version in dependency order (cleaning up the footage first, because everything else rests on it) and ends with it in your wife's hands for two weeks. Part IV takes what those two weeks taught you and adds the rest: her look, cutting to the beat, talking-head videos, big files. Part V is the version you could sell. Part VI is reference: things to look up, not read through.

Each stage ends with something concrete: a folder, a video that plays, a sheet of stills, a reel on a phone. When a stage says "you now have X," check that you do before moving on.

01

The big picture

What the tool is, who it is for, and the pipeline you are building.

In one sentence: she attaches clips from her camera roll to a chat in the Claude app, says what she wants, and Claude sends back a finished reel: the best moments, in a good order, the right shape for the platform, with music. No laptop, no second app, no timeline to drag.

Two shapes, one tool

It makes video in two shapes, and both are first-class from the first day of the build. Tall (written 9:16; 1080 by 1920 pixels) is for short-form: reels, shorts, the feed. Wide (16:9; 1920 by 1080) is for longer-form YouTube, which for you mostly means talking-head footage shot sideways and kept that way. The name says "reel," but widescreen is not an afterthought. The machinery is the same and only the frame differs, so nothing in the code is allowed to assume tall.

What you are actually building

You already proved the idea twice: once in Claude Code with clips in a folder, and once on her phone ("join these two clips into one vertical video" worked). In both tests Claude made up the editing commands on the spot. That works, and it has three problems. It does the job a little differently every time. It repeats the same mistakes (sideways clips, washed-out colour) until someone notices. And it knows nothing about her taste. Vid2Reel is those lessons written down once and reused:

  • A toolbox of small programs that already know how to handle iPhone footage: clean it up, take stills from it, cut it, add music, save it in the right shape.
  • An editor's handbook, in plain English, that tells Claude how to use the toolbox and what a good reel looks like for her.
  • A small music library that is legal to use.
  • A taste profile for each social account: a short section in the instructions of the Claude Project that account already has, saying how reels for that account should look. She posts to several accounts, so she has several profiles. If you post with it too, yours sit in your own Projects. The toolbox and the handbook are shared.
  • A skill in her Claude account that connects all of it to her chats. A skill is a short instruction file Claude reads when a request matches it. You have built these before (your FAT report is one).

The skill and a profile are easy to blur, and they are different things. The skill is the engine. It is installed once in a Claude account and works in every chat in that account, inside a Project or not. A profile is taste. It sits in one Project's instructions, and only chats in that Project see it. So Vid2Reel is still a skill; her Projects only tell it how each account likes its reels.

Where the work happens

When "Code execution and file creation" is switched on in a Claude account, every chat gets a private, temporary computer in Anthropic's cloud. Files attached to the chat appear on it, Claude can run programs on it, and it is wiped afterwards. This guide calls it the sandbox. Her test worked because the sandbox already has FFmpeg on it, the free program that does nearly all video cutting and converting behind the scenes. Vid2Reel runs entirely in the sandbox. That is what "no computer needed" means in practice: the computer is Anthropic's, and it comes with her Claude plan.

This also explains the video helper you set up on your Mac this week (an MCP server: a helper program that gives Claude extra tools on one particular computer). It is the same idea living in the wrong place. It runs on your Mac, so it can't follow her phone. This project puts the ability where her phone can reach it.

Why people would pay (and why nobody does yet)

Version one is her tool first, and yours too if you want it for your own posts, which costs almost nothing extra because of how taste is kept (Stage 17). Nobody pays, there is no sign-in and no website. The question this build answers is not "will they pay" but "does she post the reel without opening another editor?" That is the only currency at this stage, and Stage 20 measures it. You decided on October 11 to defer the sellable version; Stage 27 says what it would take and what carries over.

Why this is not your usual pipeline

Your other apps go Mac, GitHub, Vercel, users, because they are websites. This one has no website. Claude's chat is the screen, the sandbox is the server, and her Claude plan is the hosting. So Vercel, the database and sign-in all sit out, and GitHub takes on a second job: when you push a change, her phone's Claude picks it up the next time she makes a reel. You settled that on October 12, and Stage 10 records it.

What is the brain?

Claude, at the moment she uses it. The Claude in her chat looks at stills from her clips, decides which moments to use and in what order, and runs the toolbox to build the video. This is the opposite of your other apps, which need no AI once they are built. Two things follow. The toolbox itself contains no AI and calls no AI. It is ordinary programs that measure and cut. All the judgement comes from the Claude she is already talking to, guided by the handbook. And there is no Claude API account, no key and no per-use bill. It runs on her normal Claude plan, and each reel uses some of that plan's allowance (Stage 07).

The one thing to protect

A reel worth posting, made entirely from her phone. The result comes first. You loosened this on October 11: you would rather she answer three to five questions that make the reel good than answer one and get something lame she can't use. So the tool is allowed to ask, on two conditions: every question has to change the reel that comes back, and each has to be answerable in a few words. What stays fixed is the phone. If a feature for reels needs a computer, a second app, or a setting she has to remember, it is wrong, however clever it is. Every stage has to pass through that gate. You made one exception on October 12: a long video for YouTube is edited on a Mac from start to finish, because a phone chat cannot hand back a file that size (Stage 23).

The hard part

Claude cannot watch video. It can look at still pictures. So the toolbox has to turn each clip into a few sheets of stills with the time printed on every one, plus measurements a still can't show (how much is moving, how shaky the camera is, how loud it is). From those, Claude has to pick moments it never saw in motion. Getting that good enough to trust is Stage 15, and it is where the build is won or lost. The second hard part is plainer, and Stage 11 has now measured it: footage goes into a phone chat without trouble, but nothing larger than 30 MiB (about 31 MB) comes back out. Short reels fit. A long widescreen video does not, and Stage 23 deals with that.

Decisions that are expensive to reverse

Bake these in from the first prompt and the first CLAUDE.md (the instruction file Claude Code reads at the start of every session; Stage 02). Each is a sentence now and a rewrite later.

  • Every edit is a cut list first. Claude never cuts video directly. It writes a short list (which clip, from what second to what second, in what order, what music) and one program builds exactly what the list says. "Make it shorter" then means changing two numbers and rebuilding, not starting over. Editors call this list an EDL, an edit decision list; this guide calls it the cut list.
  • Three jobs that never mix. One part of the code looks (measures clips, makes stills) and never decides anything. Claude decides and writes the cut list. One part renders (builds the video from the list) and never decides anything. Stage 05 has the rule in full.
  • One working format. Every clip is first converted to the same shape and speed (Stage 14). Everything after that step assumes it. iPhone footage is too varied to handle any other way.
  • Her originals are never changed. The toolbox works on copies and every result is a new file.
  • It must run in the sandbox as it is. No paid services, no special graphics hardware, nothing that needs a website the sandbox can't reach. The sandbox can reach GitHub and the standard places programs are installed from, and little else.
  • The phone is the truth. Your Mac is the workshop. A stage is not finished until a reel has been made from a phone with that stage's code.
  • Her footage never goes on GitHub. The repo (the project's folder plus its history, stored on GitHub) will be public, because that is how her phone fetches it. Family video stays on your Mac and her phone.
  • Only music you are allowed to hand out. Every track in the library is public domain, with its licence written next to it. "Free to download" is not the same thing (Stage 18).
  • The handbook lives in the repo, in English. How Claude should edit is a text file you can read and change yourself, saved with the code, not buried inside the skill.
  • Two shapes from the start. Tall and wide are one setting in the cut list, never two separate programs. Every command and every test covers both, so widescreen never becomes the neglected half.
  • Taste is kept per social account, outside the repo. The handbook holds what makes any reel good, plus the rules you both accept. What suits one account lives in that account's Claude Project. One tool can then serve two people, or two hundred (Stage 27), without a copy for each.
  • Every pick has a receipt. For each moment it uses, Claude notes which still it was looking at. When a reel comes out wrong, you can see why.

GSD: the workflow

Same loop as your other apps. Two steps of it mean something different here.

GSD is the planning tool that runs inside Claude Code and breaks the build into numbered phases. The five commands per phase are unchanged: /gsd-discuss-phase, /gsd-plan-phase, /gsd-execute-phase, /gsd-verify-work, /gsd-ship. /gsd-next when unsure, /gsd-quick for small things, /gsd-debug for bugs. If a name has changed since this was written, type /gsd and pick from the menu.

What is special about discuss here: this is where her taste goes in. Each build stage in this guide (Stages 14 to 19) has a list of what the feature must do and a few Decide boxes. Bring that material into the discuss step, and add what the guide can't know: the reels she admires, how fast she likes cuts, what she always trims off the start of a clip. Stage 08 has you write those down with her.

What is special about verify: GSD will ask whether each feature works, and for this tool there are two different answers. "The video matches the cut list" can be proven by a test, and Claude Code can do that alone. "It's a good reel" can only be answered by a person watching it on a phone with the sound on. Never accept the first as a substitute for the second.

Nothing to do yet; you start GSD in Stage 12, once the project exists and has made a reel from a phone.

Watch out

Do not verify a video stage by watching the result on the Mac's big screen. A reel is watched on a phone, held upright, usually with the sound low. Colour that looks fine on the Mac can look grey on the phone, and text that looks tidy on a monitor can sit under the platform's buttons. Verify means: the file saved to a phone's Photos, watched there. The one exception is a widescreen video for YouTube: watch that with the phone turned sideways and again on a computer screen, since that is where it will be seen.

Free open-source code: borrow parts, not whole projects

You asked to use free GitHub projects where it makes sense. It makes sense for parts: FFmpeg and the libraries above are exactly that, and later stages add two more (one that finds the beat in a song, one that turns speech into text). Most assume special graphics hardware or paid services the sandbox doesn't have, and you would inherit a pile of code neither you nor Claude Code chose. One architecture rule

The judgement is the product; the cutting is machinery. Insist from day one on three jobs that never mix:

  • v2r/look/ is the eyes. It takes clips and produces facts: sheets of stills with times printed on them, and a file of measurements. It never chooses anything.
  • The cut list is the decision. It is plain data, written at the time by the Claude in her chat, following EDITOR.md. No program in the repo writes one.
  • v2r/render/ is the hands. It takes a cut list and builds exactly that video. It never improves, trims or reorders on its own.

If this holds, a better way of seeing footage next year (a model that can watch video, say) replaces only look. A website for paying customers (Stage 27) is a new front door that produces the same cut lists. And a wrong reel can always be traced: either the facts were wrong, the decision was wrong, or the build didn't match the decision. If it doesn't hold, every one of those is a rewrite, and every bad reel is a mystery.

Say to Claude Code (in the Stage 16 discuss step, on Fable; you haven't reached it yet)
Design the cut list for Vid2Reel: a JSON file that fully describes one edit, plus a JSON Schema that checks it. It names the canvas (width, height, frames per second; tall 1080×1920 or wide 1920×1080), an ordered list of segments (which normalized clip, start and end in seconds on that clip, a horizontal focus point from 0 to 1 for cropping it to fill the canvas, an optional gentle move such as a slow push-in or pan, whether its own sound is kept), the look (a filter name that is "none" unless the user asked for one, and mild per-clip touch-up numbers that make the clips match each other), the sound (music track id from music/library.json, where in the track to start, fades, how loud music sits under kept sound), and a "why" note per segment that cites the still it was chosen from. Leave room for text overlays, speed changes, transitions and a separate voice track without building them. All times are seconds on the source clip, never frame numbers. The renderer must be able to build the video from this file and the normalized clips alone, with no other input and no choices of its own. Show me the design and one example for a 20-second tall reel and one for a wide video, in plain English, first, name what will be hardest to change later, then stop.

Normalize: any clip in, one format out

The dull stage everything else stands on. Every clip she shoots is turned into the same shape before anything looks at it.

This comes first because iPhone footage only looks uniform in the Photos app. Underneath, one clip is stored sideways with a note saying "turn me," another runs at 60 pictures a second instead of 30, a third is in HDR (a format with extra-bright colour that turns grey and flat when treated as ordinary video), and a fourth has no sound at all. A program that tries to cope with each of those at every later step will fail in a new way every week. So one step copes with all of them, once, and hands on a clean working copy.   • Name the clips. A, B, C, in the order she attached them. Those letters are how every later step, and Claude, refers to them.
  • Write a manifest: one small file listing every clip, its facts, and where its working copy is. A manifest is just a packing list.
  • Refuse politely. A file that is too big (by the limits from Stage 11) or can't be read gets one plain sentence, and the rest carry on.

The hard part. Claude can't watch a clip, so the toolbox has to show it one.

The first is a contact sheet: a grid of small stills on one picture, the way photographers used to print a whole roll of film on one page, with a label on every still such as B 07.5, meaning clip B at seven and a half seconds. The second is a file of measurements for the things a still cannot show.

What this stage must do

  • Stills at a steady rhythm, plus extras where something happens. A still every second would miss a splash that lasts a third of a second. So the code also takes a still wherever movement peaks or the scene changes.
  • Sheets Claude can read. Each still large enough to tell what is going on, the label printed on it, short clips on one sheet each. A cap on sheets per reel, because every sheet Claude looks at uses part of her plan's allowance and adds seconds.
  • Measurements, every half second: how much of the picture is changing (movement); whether it is the subject moving or the whole frame (camera shake); how sharp the picture is; how bright; how loud the sound; and whether the shot changed. What it deliberately does not do: recognise faces or objects, or say what a clip is "about." Claude does that from the stills, and does it well. The code's job is to make sure the right stills are on the sheet.

The cut list and the renderer

The first real reel: a short list of decisions, and a program that builds exactly that.

The cut list is the whole edit, written down. Here is a small one. JSON is just a strict way of writing lists so that a program can read them.

{
  "canvas":   { "width": 1080, "height": 1920, "fps": 30 },
  "look":     { "filter": "none", "touch_up": "match" },
  "segments": [
    { "clip": "C", "from": 2.0, "to": 4.5, "focus_x": 0.50,
      "why": "C 02.0 to C 04.0: wave breaks, sharp, camera steady" },
    { "clip": "A", "from": 7.5, "to": 9.0, "focus_x": 0.35,
      "why": "A 08.0: she looks up; subject left of centre" },
    { "clip": "D", "from": 0.0, "to": 3.0, "focus_x": 0.50, "move": "push-in",
      "why": "D is a photo: wide view, good closing shot" }
  ],
  "sound": { "clips": "off", "music": "golden-hour", "music_from": 6.2 }
}

Read it top to bottom: a tall frame; no filter, and a touch-up that matches the clips to each other; two and a half seconds of clip C, then a second and a half of A cropped a little left of centre because that is where she is, then three seconds of D, a photo, with a slow push-in; the clips' own sound off; a named music track, starting 6.2 seconds into it. Each "why" names the stills the choice rests on. That is the receipt from Stage 01.

What this stage must do

  • A checker for cut lists. Before anything is built, the list is checked against a rulebook (called a schema), and problems come back in plain English: "segment 3 ends at 12.4 seconds but clip B is only 9.8 seconds long."
  • A renderer that builds the list and nothing else. To render means to produce the finished video file. Its only inputs are the cut list and the working copies.
  • Every shot fills the frame. A shot that is the wrong shape is cropped, centred on its focus point. No bars and no blurred backgrounds.
  • A touch-up, never a filter. A small helper compares the clips' brightness and colour cast (measured in Stage 15) and works out a mild correction for each, so they look like one shoot. The corrections are written into the cut list as numbers and capped in settings.py, so they can only ever nudge. The filter field stays "none." Named filters arrive in Stage 21, and only when she asks.
  • Gentle movement. A segment can carry a slow push-in, pull-out or pan: a few percent over the length of the shot, with the amount capped. A photo gets one by default, since a frozen picture in a reel reads as a fault. On video it is used sparingly, and never on a shot that is already moving.
  • Tall or wide from one setting. The frame is part of the cut list: 1080 by 1920 for a tall reel, 1920 by 1080 for a wide video for YouTube. Wide is not a conversion of tall. In a wide video, footage shot sideways is shown whole, and it is the upright clip that gets cropped, with Claude saying so when that would lose most of the picture. One video is one shape throughout.
  • Sound. Each segment's own sound kept or off; a music file laid underneath (a test tone stands in until Stage 18); a short fade in, a fade out at the end; and the whole reel evened out to one standard loudness, so no reel is quieter than the one before it in her feed.
  • A file that posts cleanly. MP4, in the common H.264 and AAC formats every platform accepts, starting to play immediately, and under the size limit you measured in Stage 11. That limit is 30 MiB, and the first join overshot it for anything longer than about 40 seconds. So the renderer works backwards from the limit: it picks the quality that fits the video's length, and says so plainly when a video is too long to fit at a quality worth posting.
  • A proof sheet. Alongside the reel, a contact sheet of the finished video and a suggested cover still. Claude looks at the proof sheet before sending anything, to catch a black frame or a shot cropped onto someone's shoulder.
  • The same list always makes the same video. That is what makes golden renders (Stage 13) possible.

Left out on purpose: dissolves and other transitions, speed changes, filters, words on screen. The cut list has room for them; the renderer ignores them for now. Straight cuts are what most reels use anyway.

Why "free" is not enough

The licence that fits a public repo is CC0, a public-domain dedication: the creator has given up all rights, so anyone may use, copy and pass the work on, for any purpose, without credit. Collections released entirely under it exist (FreePD is a long-running one), and larger archives such as the Free Music Archive let you filter by licence. Two kinds of "free" music do not fit. Popular free-download sites generally allow the track in your video but forbid handing out the file itself, which is exactly what a public repo does; one widely used site's licence rules out distributing its files on their own, with nothing added. And tracks marked "attribution required" (CC BY) are free only if the creator is credited in every post. Skip both.

Cut to the beat

The single change that makes a montage feel edited: pictures change when the music does.

What this stage must do

  • Know when not to. A track with no clear beat (slow, ambient) is marked so, and its reels are left alone.

  3. Leave it off. The reel she got in Stage 19, unchanged.

Talking head

A different kind of video. The words carry it, so the edit is made on the words.

For a video of someone talking to the camera, stills say almost nothing. The useful thing to look at is a transcript: everything that was said, as text, with the time of every word. Claude reads it, decides which sentences stay, and the cuts are made at the gaps between words.

Decide: build this, or use a dedicated editor for talking-head videos

  2. Use a dedicated transcript-based video editor for these, and keep Vid2Reel for visual reels. Such editors exist, do this well, and you looked at one earlier this month. Trade-off: a second tool and a second way of working, and most are happiest on a computer.

What this stage must do

  • The shape follows where the video is going. Talking-head footage shot sideways for YouTube stays wide, 1920 by 1080, with nothing cropped. That is the default for this kind of footage. The same footage can also be cut into short tall clips for reels, cropped around the speaker, when she asks.
  • Speech to text, with a time for every word. This needs a speech-recognition model, a large file. The sandbox can only download from GitHub and the standard package sources, so the model has to come from one of those. An open-source project called sherpa-onnx publishes suitable models on GitHub, and that route has already worked in a Claude chat for you (the clip of the stringer being planed, earlier this month).

  • Where it runs. Short talking clips for reels are edited on the phone like anything else. A long talking video for YouTube is edited on the Mac (Stage 23). The commands and the handbook are the same in both places.
  • Clean cuts. Always in the gap between words, with a breath of room either side, and a tiny blend in the sound at each join so there is no click.
  • Pauses and retakes. Long silences shortened to a natural beat. When a sentence is started twice, the last complete take is kept.
  • Softening the jumps. When two pieces of the same shot meet, the picture jumps. The standard remedy is to show alternate pieces slightly closer, about eight percent, so the jump reads as a change of shot.
  • Captions. The spoken words on screen in time with the voice, a few at a time, inside the safe areas from Stage 21. On by default for these videos; she can say no.

Glossary

Plain English, this project's words only.

9:16
The tall shape of a phone held upright: 9 wide by 16 high. A reel is 1080 by 1920 pixels.
16:9
The wide shape of a television, or a phone turned sideways: 1920 by 1080 pixels. Used for longer YouTube videos.
AAC
The common format for sound inside a video file. Every platform accepts it.
B-roll
Pictures shown while a voice from somewhere else keeps talking.
Beat
The regular pulse of a piece of music, the thing you tap your foot to. A strong beat is the "one" you would count from.
Branch
A separate copy of the code where one change is built without touching the main version.
Canvas
The size and shape of the finished video: tall for reels, wide for long-form.
CC0
A public-domain dedication. The creator has given up all rights; anyone may use, copy and pass the work on without credit.
Contact sheet
A grid of small stills from a clip on one picture, each labelled with its time. How Claude "sees" video.
Cut list
The whole edit written as data: which clip, which seconds, what order, what music. Editors call it an EDL.
Doctor
The toolbox command that reports what the computer it is running on has and lacks.
Ducking
Lowering the music while someone is speaking, and raising it again after.
EDITOR.md
The editor's handbook: the plain-English file the Claude in her chat follows.
FFmpeg
The free program that reads, cuts, joins and saves video and audio. ffprobe is its companion that only reads a file's facts.
Fixture set
A folder of her real clips kept on your Mac for remaking the same test reel after every change.
Frame rate
How many pictures a second a video shows. Reels use 30. Written "fps."
Golden render
A fixed test video with its expected result written down, rebuilt after every change to catch anything that moved.
H.264
The common format for the picture inside a video file. Every phone and platform plays it.
HDR
A recording format with extra-bright colour. Looks grey if handled as ordinary video without conversion.
Manifest
The packing list: every clip in a job, its facts, and where its working copy is.
MCP server
A helper program that gives Claude extra tools on one particular computer. Not used here, because it can't follow a phone.
Normalize
Convert every clip to one standard shape, speed and colour before anything else touches it.
Project
A named space in a Claude account with its own standing instructions, which load only in chats inside it. She has one per social account, and each holds that account's taste profile.
Proof sheet
A contact sheet of the finished reel, which Claude checks before sending.
Proxy
A small stand-in copy of a clip, used for deciding the edit, with the finished video built from the original. Professional editors use them; Vid2Reel does not.
Render
Produce the finished video file from a cut list.
Repo
The project's folder plus its full history, stored on GitHub. This one is public.
Safe area
The part of a tall video the posting apps don't cover with their own buttons and captions.
Sandbox
The private, temporary computer in Anthropic's cloud behind a Claude chat. Attached files appear there, programs run there, and it is wiped afterwards.
Schema
A rulebook a data file is checked against, so a broken cut list is caught before anything is built.
Skill
A short instruction file in a Claude account that Claude reads when a request matches it. Installed once, it works in every chat in that account, inside a Project or not.
Taste profile
A short section headed "Reel style" in a Project's instructions, saying how reels for that social account should look. Claude reads it before every reel made in that Project.
Tone mapping
Converting HDR video to ordinary colour so it looks natural instead of grey.
Transcript
What was said in a clip, as text, with the time of every word.
Working copy
The normalized version of a clip that the toolbox actually uses. The original is never touched.
